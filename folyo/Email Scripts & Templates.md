Email Scripts & Templates
A collection of copy-paste email templates for every stage of the client relationship. Adapt these to your voice and situation—they're starting points, not scripts to send verbatim.

Cold Outreach Templates
The Direct Approach

Hi [Name],

[Specific observation about their business or a problem you noticed].

[Your name]

Hi [Name],

We've been customers of [company] for [time period]—[specific thing you appreciate about their product/service].

It happens that we specialize in [your service] for companies like yours. I noticed [specific opportunity or problem], and had a few ideas.

Would you be open to a quick conversation?

[Your name]

The Forgotten Lead
Most agencies only email fresh job posts. This template lets you reach out weeks or months later when competition has died down.

Hi [Name],

I saw your project from back in [month] about needing [service] to help with [main goal]. Hope it turned out well—I've been following your company since then.

If you're still looking to [main benefit], I think we can help.

Worth a quick conversation?

[Your name]

The Research-Heavy Approach
Use when you've done deep research and want to demonstrate expertise upfront.

Hi [Name],

Because you have [specific observation about their business—size, locations, recent news], I suspect you need [specific solution], which would help with [specific benefit].

Happy to share some ideas for how to approach this, but first I wanted to make sure I'm on the right track.

Does this resonate?

[Your name]

The Problem Solver
Use when you've spotted a specific, fixable problem on their site or in their business.

Hi [Name],

I noticed [specific problem—slow load time, broken feature, UX issue, etc.].

This can cause [painful consequence] and often [broader business impact].

I'd recommend [brief solution]. We use this approach with all our clients and it's saved them [benefit].

Want me to sketch out a few ideas for how to fix this?

[Your name]

Day 14 (The Magic Email):

Since I haven't heard from you on this, I have to assume your priorities have changed.

That's it. No sign-off, no extra text. The abruptness gets responses.

After Positive Response (Moving to Close)
Confirming timeline:

Great to hear! Are you looking to kick this off in the next month or so?

If yes, schedule a call:

Perfect. The best way to figure out the right approach would be a quick 15-minute call to discuss what you need.

Does [Day] at [Time] work?

If they're not ready yet:

No problem—my best clients usually take their time too.

When you are ready, would you be looking for something [faster/bigger/different] than what you have now?

I'll follow up in [timeframe] unless I hear from you sooner.

Objection Handlers
"We're not ready yet"
Totally understand. My job is to give you what you need so when you are ready, you don't waste any time.

When you do move forward, would you be looking for something [faster/more robust/different] than what you have now?

"Can you send your portfolio?"
Instead of blasting your full portfolio, tailor it:

Absolutely. Rather than send everything, I'll pull together 2-3 projects most relevant to what you're working on.

Quick question first: is [specific outcome] the main priority, or is there something else driving this project?

"We're looking at other options"
Makes sense—you should. A few things that might help as you compare:

[One specific differentiator about how you work]

Happy to answer any questions as you decide. What would be most helpful to know?

"Your price is too high"
I hear you. We've found that clients in your situation sometimes regret going with the cheaper option because [specific consequence—scope creep, timeline issues, etc.].

That said, if budget is the priority, I can suggest a smaller scope that gets you [80% of the result] for [lower price]. Want me to put that together?

Client dragging their feet
I suspect you might be viewing this project as a cost rather than an investment in [specific business outcome].

If [project type] is important to your business, doing it right matters—and there's nobody better to do it than us.

If you'd prefer to start smaller, I'm happy to recommend a phased approach. What would be most helpful?

After verbal agreement, before contract
Great—excited to get started.

I'll send over the agreement and invoice today. Once the deposit is received, I'll block off time in our schedule and we can kick off [specific first step].

Any questions before I send it over?

When you're fully booked
Thanks for reaching out—this sounds like a great project.

Quick heads up: we're currently booking [X weeks/months] out. If you need someone immediately, I'm happy to recommend another agency. But if you'd like to work with us specifically, the sooner we get a signed agreement, the sooner I can lock in your spot.

Only signed contracts with deposits hold a place in our schedule.

Want to set up a call to discuss the details?

Send this after you've received a testimonial:

Upselling a Retainer
When a client asks about ongoing support or future changes:

For ongoing updates like that, we offer a monthly retainer. Some of our clients pay $[X] per month to have us on call for up to [X] hours—that time is reserved just for them, no matter what.

Without a retainer, we can still handle updates at our standard rate, but you'd need to wait in our queue if we're booked with other projects.

For a company your size, I'd recommend the retainer—we can sometimes be booked weeks out, and I know you'll want changes done quickly.

Want me to send over the details?

Email Endings That Work
Never end with "let me know what you think." Instead, try:

Can I send you a few ideas?

Does that sound worth exploring?

Would a quick call this week help?

Does [Day] at [Time] work?

Want me to send more info?

Sound good?

Is this still a priority?

Worth a conversation?

Should I put together a proposal?

Can I send over the next steps?

The goal: they can reply "yes" or "sounds good" without thinking.

## Why We're Doing This

We have no active client work right now. The best use of your time is building a portfolio piece that helps us close the next client. This is a sales asset, not a personal project.

Landing page and homepage design is your strength. This brief is designed around that. We are going to take that strength and turn it into something that speaks directly to the founders we are trying to reach.

When a Series A fintech founder sees this, they should think: "these designers understand our world." That is the bar. Not just a beautiful page — a page that feels right for the audience.

## The Company — Vaulted

Vaulted is a fictional but realistic B2B SaaS product that helps finance teams at early-stage startups automate financial compliance and stay audit-ready year-round. Think of the pain a Series A startup feels in the 3 months before a fundraise or audit — scrambling to organise financial records, chase down documentation, and prove to investors that their books are clean. Vaulted eliminates that scramble.

**What Vaulted does**

▸ Connects to a company's existing financial tools (QuickBooks, Stripe, Mercury, Brex)

▸ Automatically organises and categorises financial records for audit readiness

▸ Surfaces compliance gaps before they become investor or auditor problems

▸ Generates audit-ready reports in one click — no accountant needed for the basics

▸ Keeps a real-time compliance health score visible to the CFO and CEO

**Who buys it**

▸ Primary buyer: CFO or Head of Finance at a Series A/B startup (20-120 employees)

▸ Also relevant to: CEO at an early-stage company without a dedicated finance hire

▸ They are time-poor, number-literate, and deeply sceptical of software that overpromises

▸ They have been burned before by tools that required too much setup or too much manual work

▸ Trust, credibility, and simplicity are the three things that will make them convert

## The Design Challenge

Fintech SaaS homepages have a problem. Most of them look identical — dark navy background, glowing purple gradients, abstract 3D blobs, and a headline that says something like "The future of financial operations." They feel like they were designed by the same template.

Vaulted needs to stand out by doing the opposite: **clarity over style**. The buyer is a CFO. They are not impressed by visual tricks. They want to understand what the product does in 5 seconds, trust that the company is credible in 10, and know how to get started in 15.

**The three things this homepage must do**

1. **Communicate the value proposition immediately** — what it is, who it is for, what problem it solves
2. **Build trust fast** — logos, numbers, social proof, and a product that looks real and polished
3. **Create a clear conversion path** — one obvious next step, repeated at the right moments

## Page Sections — Full Homepage Flow

Design the full page as a continuous scroll. Every section leads into the next. There is a narrative arc here — hook, explain, prove, detail, reassure, convert. Follow it.

### Section 1: Navigation Bar — Orient and build immediate trust

**Content**
Fixed top navigation. Vaulted wordmark on the left. Nav links: Product, Integrations, Pricing, Customers. Right side: Login link + primary CTA button (Start free trial or Book a demo).

**Design notes**
Keep it clean and minimal. No mega-menus. The nav should communicate maturity and confidence without being busy. A subtle border or shadow separates it from the hero on scroll.

**Must include**

▸ Vaulted wordmark — design a simple, professional logotype (not a generic icon)

▸ 4 nav links maximum

▸ Login + CTA button in top right

▸ Mobile: hamburger menu with full-screen nav overlay

▸ Sticky behaviour on scroll — indicate this in your design notes

### Section 2: Hero Section — Hook the right person in under 5 seconds

**Content**
The most important section on the page. Headline, subheadline, primary CTA, and a product visual. The headline must be specific and outcome-focused — not vague and aspirational. Use: "Always audit-ready. No last-minute scramble." or similar. Subheadline explains who it is for and what it connects to.

**Design notes**
The product visual is critical. Do not use an abstract illustration or a 3D blob. Show a real UI — a dashboard screenshot or a mockup of Vaulted's compliance health screen. Founders want to see the product, not a metaphor for it. The hero should feel confident and direct, not loud.

**Must include**

▸ Headline: outcome-focused, specific, under 8 words

▸ Subheadline: who it is for + what it does, 1-2 sentences

▸ Primary CTA button — "Start free trial" or "Book a demo" — high contrast

▸ Secondary CTA — "See how it works" or "Watch a 2-min demo" — lower visual weight

▸ Product UI mockup or dashboard screenshot — must look real

▸ Social proof line below the CTAs — e.g. "Trusted by 200+ finance teams" or logos

### Section 3: Logo Bar / Social Proof Strip — Remove scepticism immediately

**Content**
A simple horizontal strip showing logos of recognisable companies or tools Vaulted integrates with or is trusted by. Either customer logos (Acme Corp, Luma, etc.) or integration logos (QuickBooks, Stripe, Mercury, Brex, Xero). A short label above: "Trusted by finance teams at" or "Connects with your existing stack".

**Design notes**
This section does one job: make the CFO feel like other serious companies use this. Keep it visually quiet — greyscale logos, clean spacing, no boxes or cards. Let the logos speak.

**Must include**

▸ 6-8 logos, greyscale, evenly spaced

▸ One-line label above the logos

▸ No borders, no cards, no drop shadows — just logos on the background

▸ Mobile: logos scroll horizontally or wrap to two rows

### Section 4: Problem — Agitate the Pain — Make the reader feel seen before you sell

**Content**
A section that names the problem before presenting the solution. Use a two or three column layout showing the "before Vaulted" state — the chaos, the scramble, the spreadsheets at 11pm before an investor meeting. Each column names a specific pain point with a short description.

**Design notes**
This is an empathy section. It is not about Vaulted yet. It is about the reader nodding along. The tone should be direct and slightly wry — "Sound familiar?" energy. Use simple icons or minimal illustrations, not photography.

**Must include**

▸ Section headline: frames the problem, not the solution

▸ 2-3 pain point columns or cards

▸ Each card: icon + short headline + 1-2 sentence description

▸ Tone: empathetic and specific, not dramatic

▸ Subtle background colour change from the hero to signal a new section

### Section 5: Product Overview — How It Works — Explain the product without a wall of text

**Content**
A step-by-step or feature walkthrough section. Show how Vaulted works in 3-4 steps: Connect your tools, Vaulted organises your records, Get your compliance score, Generate your audit report. Each step has a number, a short headline, a description, and a supporting product visual or icon.

**Design notes**
Alternate layout works well here — step on the left, product visual on the right, then flip. Or a simple numbered list with product screenshots as supporting visuals. The product visuals must look like real screens — design them at enough fidelity that they are believable.

**Must include**

▸ 3-4 clearly numbered steps

▸ Each step: number + headline + 2-3 sentence description + supporting visual

▸ Product visuals for at least 2 of the steps — real-looking UI, not placeholder boxes

▸ Clear visual progression from step to step

▸ Mobile: steps stack vertically cleanly

### Section 6: Features — What You Get — Give depth to buyers who want to go further

**Content**
A feature grid or card layout showing Vaulted's core capabilities. 6-8 features. Each has an icon, a short name, and a one-sentence description. Features include: Real-time compliance score, One-click audit reports, Automated record categorisation, Integrations with 20+ tools, Role-based access for your team, Audit trail and version history, Custom compliance rules, Slack and email alerts.

**Design notes**
This section is for the buyer who is already interested and wants specifics before booking a demo. Keep it clean and scannable — a grid of cards, consistent icon style, tight copy. Do not make it look like a comparison table.

**Must include**

▸ 6-8 feature cards in a 2 or 3 column grid

▸ Each card: icon + name + one sentence

▸ Consistent icon style throughout — all outline, all filled, or all illustrated — pick one

▸ Subtle card borders or soft shadows — no heavy drop shadows

▸ Mobile: 1 column stack

### Section 7: Social Proof — Customer Quote or Case Study Callout — Prove it with a real voice

**Content**
One or two customer testimonials or a single highlighted case study stat. A quote from a CFO or finance lead with their name, title, company, and headshot placeholder. Optionally, a large stat callout: "94% of Vaulted customers pass their first audit" or "Finance teams save 12 hours per month on compliance prep".

**Design notes**
One strong quote beats three weak ones. Make the testimonial feel human and specific — not generic corporate praise. The name and company give it weight. The headshot placeholder signals that a real person said this. Use a visually distinct background for this section to make it stand out.

**Must include**

▸ 1-2 testimonial quotes — specific, outcome-focused, attributed

▸ Name, title, company for each quote

▸ Circular headshot placeholder

▸ Optional: 1-2 large stat callouts alongside the quotes

▸ Visually distinct background — dark, light, or brand-coloured

### Section 8: Pricing Section — Remove the last objection before the CTA

**Content**
A simple 3-tier pricing layout. Starter, Growth, Enterprise. Each tier has a name, a monthly price, a short positioning line, a list of 5-6 included features, and a CTA button. The middle tier (Growth) is visually highlighted as the recommended plan.

**Design notes**
Pricing pages make or break conversion for B2B SaaS. Keep it honest and scannable. No hidden fee asterisks. No confusing feature comparisons. The CFO buyer will read this carefully — respect their time. The highlighted plan should feel like a genuine recommendation, not a manipulative upsell.

**Must include**

▸ 3 pricing tiers in a card layout

▸ Centre card visually emphasised — border, background, or "Most popular" badge

▸ Each tier: name + price + positioning line + feature list + CTA button

▸ Annual vs monthly billing toggle

▸ Enterprise tier: "Contact us" CTA instead of a price

▸ Short reassurance line below the cards: "No credit card required" or "14-day free trial"

▸ Mobile: cards stack vertically

### Section 9: Footer CTA + Footer Navigation — Close with a clear next step

**Content**
Two parts. First, a full-width CTA band just above the footer — a bold closing statement and a repeated primary CTA button. Something like: "Stop dreading your next audit. Start free today." Then the footer itself: logo, navigation links organised by category, social links, legal links, and a short company description.

**Design notes**
The closing CTA band is your last chance to convert a reader who scrolled the whole page. Make it feel decisive and low-friction. The footer should be clean and organised — not a dumping ground. Four or five link columns maximum.

**Must include**

▸ Full-width CTA band: bold headline + primary CTA button + optional secondary CTA

▸ Footer: Vaulted wordmark + tagline

▸ Footer nav columns: Product, Company, Resources, Legal

▸ Social icons: LinkedIn, Twitter/X

▸ Copyright line

▸ Mobile: footer columns stack to 2 columns then 1

## Copy Guidance

You are responsible for writing the placeholder copy in this design. It does not need to be perfect — Asaad will refine it — but it must be directionally right. Bad placeholder copy makes even great design look weak.

**Rules for writing the copy in this design**

▸ Be specific, not vague — "Automated compliance for Series A startups" beats "The future of finance"

▸ Write for a CFO — outcome-focused, number-literate, sceptical of hype

▸ Avoid buzzwords: seamless, powerful, next-generation, cutting-edge, game-changing

▸ Every headline should answer: what is it, who is it for, or why does it matter

▸ CTAs should state the action clearly — "Book a demo" or "Start free trial" not "Get started"

▸ Feature names should be short, plain nouns — "Audit Report Generator" not "Intelligent Reporting Suite"

## Design Direction

**Tone**
Professional and direct. This is software for serious finance people at growing companies. It should feel confident and clean — like the product itself is well-organised. Not cold, not corporate, not startup-cute.

**Visual style**

▸ Clean, generous whitespace — do not cram the page

▸ Neutral base: white or very light grey background, dark navy for text

▸ One primary brand colour for CTAs and accents — a strong, trustworthy blue or teal

▸ Typography: a clean, modern sans-serif — Inter, Sohne, or similar

▸ Product UI mockups must look like real software — design them at real fidelity

▸ Icons: consistent style throughout — outline or filled, pick one and commit

▸ Photography: avoid stock photography entirely — use UI mockups and abstract data visuals instead

**Reference points to study before starting**

▸ Linear.app — for clean, confident SaaS homepage structure

▸ Brex.com — for fintech trust and credibility signals

▸ Rippling.com — for clear product storytelling in a complex B2B space

▸ Causal.app — for honest, direct copy that does not overpromise

Study these for structure and approach, not to copy the visual style.

**What to avoid**

▸ Dark navy background with glowing gradients — this is the fintech cliche, not the differentiation

▸ Abstract 3D shapes or blobs as hero visuals

▸ Generic stock photography of people in offices

▸ Overly complex animations or interactions — this is a static Figma design

▸ Inconsistent spacing — use an 8px grid throughout

▸ More than two typefaces
