# 10 Tips for Winning More Projects

After 12 years running Folyo and reviewing over 5,000 RFPs, I've watched hundreds of agencies win and lose projects. These are the 10 most important things I've learned about sending more persuasive proposals in less time.

## 10. Eliminate Time-Waster RFPs

Not every RFP deserves a proposal. Your time is limited, and some projects are designed to waste it.

Skip RFPs that have: 60 pages of legalese and compliance requirements (these organizations move slowly and often favor incumbents), unrealistic scope with low budgets ("complete redesign, CRM integration, mobile app, and SEO — budget: $15K"), or kitchen-sink requirements that signal they don't know what they actually need.

I've seen agencies spend 20 hours responding to these, only to lose to a vendor who was always going to win. Learn to spot them early and move on.

## 9. Use the Q&A Window

Most formal RFPs include a Q&A period where agencies can submit questions before the deadline. I'm consistently surprised by how few agencies use it.

This isn't just about getting information. It's an opportunity to stand out, qualify the opportunity based on how they respond, and start building a relationship before you even submit.

One agency I work with asks: "Would it be okay to reach out between now and [deadline] with more questions as needed?" Easy for the client to say yes — and now they have permission to follow up, which most competitors won't do.

## 8. Optimize Your Follow-Ups

Don't just follow up after you submit your proposal. Follow up at every stage of the process — email, phone, text, whatever works.

The agencies that win aren't necessarily better at writing proposals. They're better at staying in front of the client throughout the entire process. A simple "just checking in on timing" keeps you top of mind when most of your competitors submitted their PDF and disappeared.

**A simple follow-up email that works:**

> Subject: Quick follow-up on [Project Name] proposal
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Just wanted to check in on timing for the [Project Name] decision. We're still very interested in working with you on this.
>
> Is there any additional information I can provide that would be helpful?
>
> [Your name]

Nothing clever. Just staying present while everyone else goes silent.

## 7. Frame Your Proposal With Metrics

Don't make your proposal about you or your work. Make it about the outcome the client wants.

Instead of "here's our portfolio, isn't it great?" — show metrics. How much did conversions increase after launch? How many hours per week did your CMS save the client's team? What was the revenue impact?

The best agencies I've seen do this well (like Kurt Elster at Ethercycle) lead with the business results, not the design work. The client cares about what the project will do for them, not how talented you are.

## 6. Track Your Pipeline

When you're sending 10 proposals per month, you'll lose track without a system.

You don't need fancy software — a spreadsheet works. Track: the RFP and organization, submission deadline, your status (evaluating / pursuing / submitted / won / lost), follow-up dates, and notes on what happened.

The discipline of tracking is what matters, not the tool. It's how you know who to follow up with and when, and it's how you start to see patterns in what's working.

## 5. Do Enough Volume

Here's something most agencies don't want to hear: from the data I've tracked, it takes roughly 10 proposals per month for about 6 months for an agency to land a six-figure contract.

That's not a guarantee — it's an average. But the math matters.

The agencies I've watched struggle send 2-3 proposals per month, spend way too long on each one, and wonder why nothing's landing. The agencies that win treat proposals like a numbers game and keep sending even when they get ghosted.

## 4. Create Reusable Proposal Components

You can't spend 8 hours on every proposal and still hit your volume targets. The solution is building a library of reusable components.

Save your best case studies, team bios, process explanations, and pricing structures. Use proposal software (or even just a folder of templates) so you can assemble proposals quickly and spend your customization time on the parts that matter — the stuff specific to this client's situation.

This won't be 100% drag-and-drop every time, but it should cut your proposal time significantly.

## 3. Write Your Proposal Like a Sales Letter

There's a science to writing persuasive copy, and your proposal should use it.

One framework that works well: **Pain-Agitation-Solution.**

▸ **Pain:** Acknowledge their problem in their words. ("You mentioned your current site isn't converting visitors into leads.")

▸ **Agitation:** Make it feel urgent. ("Every month this continues, you're leaving revenue on the table.")

▸ **Solution:** Position your offer as the answer. ("We'll rebuild your site with conversion as the primary goal.")

This takes 30 seconds to read but immediately shows you understand their situation — which most proposals don't.

## 2. Focus on the Pain Behind the RFP

Every RFP exists because someone has a problem they need solved. The more your proposal speaks to that pain, the more compelling it becomes.

Don't just respond to their technical requirements. Try to understand why they're doing this project. What's broken? What's the business impact? What does success look like to the person who will decide?

If you can get them on a call to dig into this, even better. The agencies that redefine the scope based on the client's actual goals naturally position themselves as the best fit — because they helped define what "best" means.

## 1. Go Beyond Your Proposal

This is the most important thing I've learned from watching agencies win and lose: the proposal is not as important as you think.

One agency told me they won an $80K project primarily because of a LinkedIn message. When they asked the client what sealed the deal, the client said: "Your proposal was great, but what really sold me was that you followed up. You asked questions that showed you actually understood what we were trying to do."

Sometimes the entire RFP process is a formality. The organization has to solicit proposals, but they already know who they want. Your job is to be that agency — the one they already trust before proposals are even due.

Call them. Connect on LinkedIn. Ask good questions. Follow up more than once. Most of your competitors will submit a PDF and hope for the best. That's your advantage.

**A simple LinkedIn message that opens doors:**

> Hi [Name], I saw your RFP for [Project] and my agency is putting together a proposal. I had a quick question about [specific thing from the RFP] — would you be open to a brief call this week to discuss?

That's it. You're not trying to be clever. You're trying to start a conversation that 90% of your competitors will never attempt.
